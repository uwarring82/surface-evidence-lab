Surface Evidence Lab

Two microscope images look different. Has the surface changed, has the measuring tip changed, or are we looking at a different place? This lab helps you work out what the evidence can actually tell you.

You will read published experiments, compare images and explain your reasoning. These microscopes move a tiny probe tip across a surface and turn its measured response into an image. A bright patch or a stripe needs interpretation; its appearance alone does not tell us what it is made of.

Start with the guided tasks below. You can begin with the supplied readings and a place to take notes; no coding or microscope operation is required. Tutors help set the scope and discuss what the evidence supports.

Start with one image sequence Open the worksheet

Start here: one sequence, one careful claim

New to surface microscopy? Begin with this short practice task using Croshaw Fig. 5, which is freely available. You do not need to understand the whole paper before taking the first step.

  1. Open the figure and choose one row. Use the figure sheet. Pick (a–c), (d–f) or (g–i), and read its caption and panel settings. Each row is a separate sequence.
  2. Write down what you can see. Note two visible features or changes, with panel letters. Check the scale bars, scan arrows and displayed ranges. For now, describe the image without assigning a cause.
  3. Read what the authors think happened. Read the Fig. 5 discussion on p. 1356 of the Croshaw paper. Keep their explanation separate from your observations. List unfamiliar words to discuss with your tutor.
  4. Try one row of the claim table. Use the template to make a claim about the same sample, the same place, or what happened between images. Add its source and one limit. Write “not reported” when information is missing.
  5. Compare your reasoning with a partner or tutor. Explain what supports your claim and what further evidence might change your mind. Bring the annotated sequence and your table row; a well-explained uncertainty is a useful result.

For tutors: agree the task first

Choose the questions, available time and expected output together. This starter is practice; it does not replace the full core worksheet or its claim-table requirements.

Before Part A, check access to Seibert. Q2 and Q4 need the paper. If access fails, use the instructor guidance and record what remains only partly answerable.

Let learners attempt an answer before opening the instructor notes, which contain answers. Discuss the evidence and assumptions, rather than rewarding how many optional tasks they attempt.

Choose how far to take the question

You do not need to complete every level. Guided core work is a worthwhile endpoint. The later levels are optional choices for more independent work, not a checklist for every student. Agree your scope with your tutor; more freedom changes the task, not the standard of evidence.

Level 1 · Beginner · Guided core

Work from the supplied evidence

After the starter, use the worksheet to compare explanations and separate claims about the sample, the place and its history.

Do
Work through core Parts A and B (Q1–Q10) and the claim table, in the order agreed with your tutor.
Boundaries
Use the selected papers, figures and source locators. Keep the protocols separate. Do not fill gaps with guessed dates, counts or experimental steps.
Bring back
Your answers and a table covering all three relationship kinds, with at least one supported and one genuinely unresolved relationship. Unknown chemical identity alone does not meet that last requirement.
Stop when
You have completed the agreed core work and discussed its limits. Optional readings, calculations and research tasks are not required to finish this level.

Level 2 · Advanced · Optional

Choose one question to investigate further

You choose the comparison or method within a bounded question. For example, examine the time claims in Part C, compare the differently scoped interpretations in Part D, or design a control for one core case.

Tasks, boundaries and a useful result
Do
Choose one extension, state the question and decide what evidence would distinguish the possible answers. A calculation or analysis script is an option when it helps answer that question.
Boundaries
Agree the sources or data, method and time limit before starting. Check that needed data are accessible. Label a proposed experiment as a proposal and an illustrative calculation as a model, rather than as a measured result.
Bring back
A short analysis with source locators, assumptions, steps someone else could repeat, and a conclusion that states what remains unresolved.
Stop when
You have answered the bounded question or reached the agreed limit. Record missing evidence or a null result; discuss a new scope before expanding the search.

Level 3 · Open research · Optional

Frame your own research problem

At the widest scope, the question, material and method are open. You might design a new experiment, develop an image-comparison method, study another surface, or build a way to document a specimen’s history. These are project directions, not established results or required coursework.

Broad scope, with an explicit research plan
Do
Define a physical or evidence question that matters, explain why it is not already answered, and propose observations that could support or challenge your explanation. Choose tools only after the question is clear.
Boundaries
With a supervisor, turn the broad aim into a feasible first milestone: needed data or equipment, access and permissions, time, resources and a way to test the method. Practical experiments require an agreed lab arrangement.
Bring back
First, a research proposal with alternatives and success criteria. If work proceeds, retain the original data, analysis steps, uncertainties and changes of interpretation so others can assess the result.
Stop when
The agreed milestone is reached or feasibility fails. An explained limit can justify revising or ending a project. Completing the teaching packet alone does not validate a new research method.

Scientific rigour matters at every level

  • Make claims traceable. Give the paper, page, panel or data record that supports each important statement.
  • Separate observation from explanation. Say what you saw, what the authors report, and what you infer. Identify a proposal or assumption as such.
  • Check comparisons. Look at units, scale bars, colour ranges and experimental conditions before comparing images or numbers.
  • Keep uncertainty visible. Do not invent missing information. Consider another explanation and say what evidence would help distinguish it.
  • Keep the reasoning checkable. Record your steps and assumptions, including for calculations or code, and explain why a conclusion follows.
  • Respect the source. Credit reused material and check its reuse terms. Clearly label your own notes, changes and interpretations.

A modest claim with clear evidence is valuable. “The available evidence does not decide this” can be a strong conclusion when you explain why.

Four claims that are easy to blur

An image pair can support a mechanism and still leave open whether it shows the same specimen, the same place or an undisturbed history. Students record each as a separate claim, with its source and what remains unresolved.

MechanismPhysical explanation
Which process accounts for what is seen: adsorbed gas or contamination; hydrogen removal, a changed tip, or both?
RelationshipSpecimen identity
Do two observations concern the same physical sample?
RelationshipSpatial correspondence
Do they show the same place on that sample? How precisely can the locations be matched?
RelationshipState history
What happened to the specimen in between, including changes caused by the measurement itself?

The same location can be in a changed state. A later interpretation of a comparable phenomenon does not overwrite an earlier claim about a different specimen.
